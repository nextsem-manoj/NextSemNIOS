## Section 1: Number Formation, Place Value & Systems

### 🔹 Example & In-Text Questions

#### **Q1. (Pattern of Smallest & Largest Numbers)**
Complete the sequence and state the relationship between largest $n$-digit numbers and smallest $(n+1)$-digit numbers:
1. Largest 3-digit number $+ 1 =$ Smallest 4-digit number: $999 + 1 = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}$
2. Largest 4-digit number $+ 1 =$ Smallest 5-digit number: $9,999 + 1 = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}$
3. Largest 5-digit number $+ 1 =$ Smallest 6-digit number (1 Lakh): $99,999 + 1 = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}$
4. Largest 7-digit number $+ 1 =$ Smallest 8-digit number (1 Crore): $99,99,999 + 1 = \underline{\hspace{2cm}}$

> **Solution:**
> 1. $999 + 1 = 1,000$ (One Thousand)
> 2. $9,999 + 1 = 10,000$ (Ten Thousand)
> 3. $99,999 + 1 = 1,00,000$ (One Lakh — 1 followed by 5 zeroes)
> 4. $99,99,999 + 1 = 1,00,00,000$ (One Crore — 1 followed by 7 zeroes)

(b) International (American) Place Value System (with periods/commas and in words)  
(c) How many lakhs make 1 million? How many millions make 1 crore? How many lakhs make 1 billion?

> **Solution:**

#### **Q4. (Estimating Sums and Differences - Roxie vs Estu)**
Consider the addition: $4,63,128 + 4,19,682$.
1. Roxie estimates: "The sum is near 8,00,000 and is greater than 8,00,000."
2. Estu estimates: "The sum is near 9,00,000 and is less than 9,00,000."
(a) Whose estimate is closer?  
(b) Will the sum be greater than 8,50,000 or less than 8,50,000?  
(c) Calculate the exact sum.

> **Solution:**
> (a) Exact calculation: $4,63,128 + 4,19,682 = 8,82,810$.  
> Distance from 8,00,000 = $82,810$. Distance from 9,00,000 = $17,190$.  
> Therefore, **Estu's estimate ($9,00,000$) is much closer**.
>
> (b) Since $4,63,000 + 4,19,000 = 8,82,000 > 8,50,000$, the sum is **greater than 8,50,000**.
>
> (c) **Exact value:** $4,63,128 + 4,19,682 = \mathbf{8,82,810}$.

#### **Q6. (Multiplication Shortcuts for $5, 25, 125$)**
Explain and solve using regrouping/division properties:
(a) $2 \times 1768 \times 50$  
(b) $72 \times 125$ $\left[\text{Hint: } 125 = \frac{1000}{8}\right]$  
(c) $125 \times 40 \times 8 \times 25$  
(d) $25 \times 240$

> **Solution:**
> (a) $2 \times 50 \times 1768 = 100 \times 1768 = \mathbf{1,76,800}$.
>
> (b) $72 \times \frac{1000}{8} = (72 \div 8) \times 1000 = 9 \times 1000 = \mathbf{9,000}$.
>
> (c) Regroup: $(125 \times 8) \times (40 \times 25) = 1000 \times 1000 = \mathbf{10,00,000}$ (1 Million / 10 Lakhs).
>
> (d) $25 \times 240 = \frac{100}{4} \times 240 = (240 \div 4) \times 100 = 60 \times 100 = \mathbf{6,000}$.

## Section 4: Number of Digits in Products & Pattern Extensions

### 🔹 Core Rule & Investigation
> **Fundamental Theorem of Product Digits:**  
> When multiplying an **$m$-digit number** with an **$n$-digit number**, the product has either **$(m + n - 1)$ digits** or **$(m + n)$ digits**.

#### **Q7. (Predicting Product Digits)**
Determine the possible number of digits in the product of:
1. 5-digit number $\times$ 5-digit number $\rightarrow \underline{\hspace{2cm}}$ digits
2. 8-digit number $\times$ 3-digit number $\rightarrow \underline{\hspace{2cm}}$ digits
3. 12-digit number $\times$ 13-digit number $\rightarrow \underline{\hspace{2cm}}$ digits

> **Solution:**
> 1. $5 + 5 - 1 = \mathbf{9}$ or $5 + 5 = \mathbf{10 \text{ digits}}$.
> 2. $8 + 3 - 1 = \mathbf{10}$ or $8 + 3 = \mathbf{11 \text{ digits}}$.
> 3. $12 + 13 - 1 = \mathbf{24}$ or $12 + 13 = \mathbf{25 \text{ digits}}$.

## Section 5: Real-World "Did You Ever Wonder" Large Number Applications

#### **Q9. (Large Number Thought Experiments)**
1. **Mumbai in Buses:** If a bus holds 50 people, can Mumbai's population ($1.24 \text{ Crore} = 1,24,00,000$) fit into $1 \text{ Lakh}$ buses?
2. **Reaching the Moon:** If you travel $100 \text{ km/day}$, can you reach the Moon in 10 years? (Distance to Moon $= 3,84,400 \text{ km}$).
3. **Lifting Paper:** If 1 sheet of paper weighs $5 \text{ grams}$, can you lift $1 \text{ Lakh}$ sheets together?
4. **Counting 1 Million Coins:** If you count 1 coin every second without stopping, can you count 1 million coins in a day?

> **Solution:**
> 1. $1,00,000 \times 50 = 50,00,000$ (50 Lakhs). Since Mumbai's population is $1.24$ Crore ($124$ Lakhs), **No, they cannot fit**. You would need $\approx 2.48$ Lakh buses.
>
> 2. In 1 year: $100 \times 365 = 36,500 \text{ km}$.  
>    In 10 years: $36,500 \times 10 = 3,65,000 \text{ km}$.  
>    Since the distance is $3,84,400 \text{ km}$, **No, you will be short by $19,400 \text{ km}$** (takes $\approx 10.5$ years).
>
> 3. Weight $= 1,00,000 \times 5 \text{ g} = 5,00,000 \text{ g} = \mathbf{500 \text{ kg}}$ (half a ton). **No, a human cannot lift 500 kg**.
>
> 4. Total seconds in a day $= 24 \times 60 \times 60 = \mathbf{86,400 \text{ seconds}}$.  
>    In 1 day, you can only count $86,400$ coins.  
>    To count $1,000,000$ coins takes $\frac{1,000,000}{86,400} \approx \mathbf{11.57 \text{ days}}$ non-stop. **No, not in 1 day**.

#### **Exercise Q1: 10-Digit Extremes using Digits 0 to 9 Once**
Using all digits from $0-9$ exactly once (first digit cannot be 0), find:
(a) Largest multiple of 5  
(b) Smallest even number

> **Solution:**
> (a) To be the largest, digits must be in descending order $9876543210$. For a multiple of 5, the unit digit must be 0 or 5. Since 0 is already the smallest digit, putting 0 at the end gives the largest possible value:  
> **Largest multiple of 5:** $\mathbf{9,876,543,210}$.
>
> (b) For the smallest 10-digit even number: First digit must be 1 (non-zero), followed by 0, then ascending digits. The last digit must be an even number. The largest remaining even digit is 8:  
> **Smallest even number:** $\mathbf{1,023,456,798}$ (ending in even digit 8).

#### **Exercise Q3: 9-Digit Increasing Invariant Property**
> **Question:** Write a 9-digit number where exchanging *any* two digits results in a bigger number. How many such numbers exist?

> **Solution:**
> If swapping *any* pair of digits $(d_i, d_j)$ with $i < j$ always creates a strictly larger number, then every preceding digit must be strictly smaller than the subsequent digit ($d_1 < d_2 < d_3 < \dots < d_9$).  
> Since there are exactly 9 distinct non-zero digits ($1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9$), there is only **one** such strictly increasing 9-digit number:  
> **The number:** $\mathbf{123,456,789}$.  
> **Total such numbers:** Exactly $\mathbf{1}$.

#### **Exercise Q4: Strike out 10 Digits for Maximum Number**
> **Question:** Strike out 10 digits from the 20-digit number `12345123451234512345` so that the remaining 10-digit number is as large as possible.

> **Solution:**
> To make the 10-digit number as large as possible, we want the highest digits (5, 5, 5, 5...) to appear as early as possible from left to right:
> - In block 1 (`12345`), remove `1,2,3,4` (4 digits struck). Remaining starts with `5`.
> - In block 2 (`12345`), remove `1,2,3,4` (4 digits struck). Next digit is `5`.
> - Total digits struck so far = $4 + 4 = 8$. We need to strike 2 more digits from the next blocks.
> - In block 3 (`12345`), remove `1,2` (2 digits struck).
> Total struck = 10 digits.  
> Remaining number: $\mathbf{5534512345}$.

#### **Exercise Q6: Continuous Digit Stream (1, 2, 3, 4, ...)**
1. **What is the 1000th digit written?**
   - 1-digit numbers ($1-9$): $9 \text{ numbers} \times 1 = 9 \text{ digits}$ (Digits 1 to 9).
   - 2-digit numbers ($10-99$): $90 \text{ numbers} \times 2 = 180 \text{ digits}$ (Digits 10 to 189).
   - Total digits up to $99 = 189$.
   - Digits remaining to reach 1000th digit $= 1000 - 189 = 811 \text{ digits}$.
   - 3-digit numbers start from 100. $811 \div 3 = 270 \text{ with remainder } 1$.
   - $100 + 270 = \mathbf{370}$.
   - The 270th 3-digit number is 369. The next number is **370**, and remainder 1 means the **first digit** of 370, which is **`3`**.
   - **Answer:** The 1000th digit is **`3`**, occurring in the number **`370`**.

## Section 7: Matchstick & Toothpick Digital Logic Puzzles

#### **Q14. (Matchstick Digit Rules)**
Standard 7-segment display stick counts:
- `0` = 6 sticks, `1` = 2 sticks, `2` = 5 sticks, `3` = 5 sticks, `4` = 4 sticks
- `5` = 5 sticks, `6` = 6 sticks, `7` = 3 sticks, `8` = 7 sticks, `9` = 6 sticks

**Puzzles:**
1. Number **5108**: Sticks required $= 5 + 2 + 6 + 7 = \mathbf{20 \text{ sticks}}$.
2. Number **42019**: Sticks $= 4 + 5 + 6 + 2 + 6 = \mathbf{23 \text{ sticks}}$.
3. **Largest number using 24 sticks:**  
   To make the largest number, maximize the number of digits using the digit that takes the fewest sticks (`1` uses 2 sticks).  
   Number of digits $= 24 \div 2 = 12 \text{ digits}$.  
   **Largest Number:** **111,111,111,111** (Twelve 1s).
4. **Smallest number using 24 sticks:**  
   To make the smallest number, minimize the number of digits using the digit that takes the most sticks (`8` uses 7 sticks).  
   $24 = 7 + 7 + 6 + 4$ or $24 = 7 + 7 + 5 + 5 \rightarrow$ digits `8, 8, 2, 2` $\rightarrow$ **2,288** or **6,888**.
